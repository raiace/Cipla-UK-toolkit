Section 03 · Work Culture

The German Workplace

Germany has the most structured professional culture in the EMEU region. The rules are real, widely followed, and matter significantly to your professional credibility from Day 1.

01 · Non-negotiable
Punctuality — arrive 2 minutes early
Being late without calling ahead is considered disrespectful, not merely inconvenient. This applies at every level. For a German colleague, your punctuality is a direct signal of your professionalism and reliability. Call ahead even for a 5-minute delay.
02 · Core Principle
Direct communication — no subtext
If a German colleague says 'that will not work', they mean it — no diplomatic softening layer. This is not rudeness. It is efficiency and respect. Equally, praise means what it says — there is no false praise in German professional culture. Adjust: be clear, be specific, say what you mean.
03 · Language Matters
Sie vs Du — formal address
German has two forms of 'you': Sie (formal) and du (informal). Always start with Sie with colleagues, especially seniors. They will invite you to use du when the relationship is ready — this transition is a meaningful social moment. Using du too early is presumptuous.
04 · Culture Rule
Work and life are strictly separated
Working late is not dedication in Germany — it is poor planning. Emails after 6pm or calls on weekends are frowned upon in most organisations. Germany has strong employee protection laws and the culture around them is taken seriously at every level.

🇮🇳 Key differences for Indian professionals

  • Meetings have fixed agendas sent in advance — read them before attending
  • No small talk before business — brief pleasantries, then straight to the agenda
  • Titles matter — use 'Herr' / 'Frau' + last name until invited otherwise
  • Doktor counts — 'Herr Doktor Müller' not 'Mr Müller' if they have a doctorate
  • Decisions take time — German business values consensus and thoroughness
  • Never cancel a meeting at short notice — significant breach of professional trust

📧 Email & Meetings

  • Email is formal — use 'Sehr geehrte Frau/Herr [Name]' initially
  • Disagreement in meetings is healthy — Germans debate and challenge openly
  • Hierarchy is based on expertise (Fachkompetenz) as much as seniority
  • After-hours emails signal poor time management, not dedication
  • Preparing thoroughly before meetings is expected — come with data
  • Follow-up is essential — document actions and decisions in writing

Section 04 · Dining & Food Culture

Currywurst, Biergarten & Abendbrot

German food culture is more functional than social — meals are efficient, portions are generous, and the biggest surprise is that dinner is often cold bread. Not a joke.

🍞 Abendbrot: Many Germans eat a cold meal in the evening — bread, cold cuts, cheese, pickles, and spreads — rather than a hot dinner. Lunch is the main hot meal of the day. If invited to a German colleague's home for Abendbrot, expect this and appreciate it — it is a genuine cultural invitation.

🍺 Beer culture — the Biergarten rules

  • Pils (most common lager), Weißbier/Weizen (wheat beer, very Bavarian), Helles (Bavarian pale), Dunkel (dark lager)
  • Traditional Biergärten: bring your own food, buy drinks at the counter, sit communally
  • Oktoberfest (Munich, Sep–Oct): 6 million visitors — book accommodation months ahead if your assignment overlaps
  • Non-drinkers: Spezi, Apfelschorle, or alkoholfreies Bier — all completely normal and respected
  • Prost! with eye contact — both eyes — looking away while clinking is genuinely considered bad luck

🥗 Vegetarian & Indian options

  • Vegetarian: Germany is excellent — Berlin especially has outstanding vegan culture
  • Indian food: Good quality in Frankfurt (Sachsenhausen), Munich (Schwabing), Berlin (Prenzlauer Berg)
  • Halal: Widely available — especially in cities with large Turkish communities (Berlin, Frankfurt, Cologne). Döner shops are almost universally halal.
  • Pork: Central to traditional German cuisine — check ingredients in traditional restaurants
  • German cuisine: Mild by Indian standards — international restaurants in major cities offer full heat levels
Tipping in Germany: Say the total you want to pay when handing over cash — e.g. bill is €23, you hand over €30 and say 'Siebenundzwanzig bitte' (twenty-seven please). 'Stimmt so' means 'keep the change'. Do not leave coins on the table silently — it reads as dismissive.

Section 06 · Daily Living

Daily Life in Germany

German daily life has specific rules that genuinely differ from India, Spain, or Brazil. The Sunday rule and the recycling system catch almost every first-time visitor off guard.

🛒 Groceries

  • Rewe (premium everyday), Edeka (quality-focused), Kaufland (large format)
  • Aldi and Lidl (both German) — excellent budget value
  • Alnatura and Basic — organic chains, popular with health-conscious Germans
  • Asian/Indian groceries: Frankfurt near Hauptbahnhof; Munich Schwabing; Berlin Neukölln
  • Online: Rewe Lieferservice, Picnic, Gorillas — set up Week 1 as Sunday backup
  • Hours: Most 7am–10pm Monday–Saturday. Closed Sunday.

📅 Sonntagsruhe — The Sunday Rule

  • Almost ALL shops closed by law on Sunday — this is strictly observed
  • Open Sundays: Petrol stations (limited groceries), bakeries (until noon), restaurants
  • Closed Sundays: All supermarkets, pharmacies (except Notapotheke), most services
  • Plan your weekly grocery shop on Saturday — no exceptions
  • Noise rules (Ruhezeit): No DIY, drilling, or loud music after 10pm or before 7am — any day
  • Your neighbours take Ruhezeit seriously. Act accordingly from Day 1.

🗑️ Germany's Recycling System — Ernst Genommen (Taken Seriously)

Getting this wrong can result in a complaint from neighbours or building management. Every German household has separate bins — this is not optional.

📱 SIM & Banking

  • SIM: Buy Day 1 at Telekom, Vodafone or O2 — bring passport. Telekom has best coverage outside cities.
  • eSIM: Available from Telekom and Congstar (Telekom subsidiary) — set up before arrival if phone supports it
  • Banking: Open N26 online before travel (use hotel address temporarily). Revolut also works.
  • Longer stays: Deutsche Bank or Commerzbank accounts require local address — 2–4 weeks to set up

📋 Anmeldung — Mandatory Registration

  • Legally required for stays over 3 months — register at local Bürgeramt within 2 weeks of arrival
  • You receive a Meldebescheinigung (registration certificate) — required for bank accounts and some rentals
  • Book Bürgeramt appointment online in advance — they fill up weeks ahead in major cities
  • HR's Global Mobility team will assist — do not attempt this alone initially

Section 07 · Housing & Healthcare

Where to Live & the Red A Cross

The Apotheke (red A cross) is your first stop for non-emergency health issues. Germany's healthcare is mandatory, comprehensive, and employer-provided. Know both before you land.

🏨 Best option: Serviced apartment

  • Fully furnished, utilities included, flexible term. Limehome, Staycity, Adagio are reliable chains.
  • Frankfurt: Westend, Nordend, Sachsenhausen — safe, well-connected, close to business
  • Munich: Schwabing, Maxvorstadt, Bogenhausen — safe, excellent metro access
  • Berlin: Mitte, Prenzlauer Berg, Charlottenburg — safe, good transport, international

🏩 Hotels

  • Marriott, Hilton, and German chains Steigenberger and Dorint — reliable, business district locations
  • Dorint and Steigenberger are Germany's premium domestic chains — consistently well-located
  • Costs usually arranged by Cipla HR for short assignments

🏠 Private Rental

  • Germany's rental market is extremely competitive — especially Munich (one of Europe's tightest)
  • Start searching 4–6 weeks before arrival with HR's support
  • Deposit (Kaution): Typically 2–3 months rent, held in separate account, returned when you leave
  • Nebenkosten: Utility costs separate from cold rent (Kaltmiete) — always check Warmmiete total
  • WG (Wohngemeinschaft): Shared flat — common in Germany, lower cost, good for meeting locals

💡 HR Global Mobility

  • Essential for Munich — market is among the most competitive in Europe
  • Anmeldung assistance — do not attempt alone initially
  • Visa, tax equalization, and lease support
  • Vetted housing contacts — avoid online scam rental listings
  • Contact 4–6 weeks before arrival — do not leave housing to the last minute
⚠️ Munich rental scams: Never transfer money without physically viewing an apartment. The Munich market is so competitive that scam listings exist. Use HR's vetted contacts only.

Healthcare in Germany

🏥 Gesetzliche Krankenversicherung (GKV)

  • Germany has mandatory health insurance — you are always covered. Employer pays ~50% of premium.
  • Major providers: TK (Techniker Krankenkasse), AOK, Barmer, DAK — all comprehensive
  • Covers: GP visits, specialists, hospital stays, prescription drugs (€5–10 co-pay)
  • Register with a Hausarzt (GP) immediately on arrival — they are your gateway to all specialist care
  • Without a GP referral, many specialists will not see you
  • Always carry your Krankenversicherungskarte (health insurance card)

💊 Apotheke — Pharmacy (Red A)

  • The red 'A' cross symbol — staffed by qualified pharmacists who advise and dispense
  • Not a supermarket pharmacy — significantly more knowledgeable than a UK chemist
  • Many OTC medications in India require a Rezept (prescription) in Germany — bring your supply
  • Notapotheke: 24-hour pharmacy open at all times — closed Apotheken list nearest open one on their door
  • Indian medicines: Bring 3-month supply — German brand names and formulations differ

Section 08 · Customs & Language

Social Customs & German Essentials

German directness is not rudeness — it is respect. And 'Prost!' with eye contact is not optional. Both eyes. Always both eyes.

01 · Norm
Privacy and personal space
Salary, relationship status, age, or religion are not appropriate conversation topics with colleagues you do not know well. Handshakes are standard — hugs reserved for close friends. Do not put your arm around a German colleague's shoulder. This is not coldness — it is respect for personal boundaries.
02 · Norm
Rules exist for a reason — and are followed
Germany is a rule-following society (Regelorientiert). Queuing is observed. Red lights are respected. Recycling bins are used correctly. Noise rules are followed. This is a social contract that everyone upholds because it makes the system work for everyone. Follow the rules visibly — your colleagues will notice.
03 · Norm
Directness is a form of kindness
A German colleague who tells you clearly that your presentation has a weak section is trying to help you — not embarrass you. Polite silence would be considered dishonest in Germany. Direct feedback is professional investment. Give direct answers when asked. Vague or diplomatic non-answers frustrate German colleagues.

Safety in Germany
